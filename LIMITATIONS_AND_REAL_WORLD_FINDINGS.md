# TradeFlow AI — technical limitations, empirical baselines and real-world boundaries

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Upfront prototype disclosure.** This project is a **constrained procurement decision prototype** built for a hackathon. It has never been validated through a closed-loop deal with a real factory or a real supplier. Every benchmark and adversarial metric comes from a **self-built sandbox and parameterised simulation calibrated against academic literature**. The decision core is a **vectorised Expectimax solver** whose validity depends strictly on its prior; the AST guardrail only constrains numbers inside a structured policy object, and the natural-language layer depends on templates and regex filtering. We believe **that in front of a technical review and engineering due diligence, drawing the boundary honestly is worth more than a perfectly self-consistent sandbox.**

### What the refactor did and did not fix

The 2026-10-14 multi-supplier refactor achieved exactly this:

| Item | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| **How quotes exist** | A hand-written 2-D array `[[15.20, ...], ...]` | Computed round by round by the adversarial agent model |

> **The accurate statement is therefore:** the decision engine's **computation contains no fake data** (the math, formulas and roofline derivations are real, and every subjective discount constant has been removed); **but the input scenario is synthetic** — supplier quotes and opponent behaviour come from our own models, **not from real transactions**. The refactor improved **auditability and dynamism**, **not** the real-world provenance of the data.

### What this project has never hidden

None of the following needed to be "caught" — it is disclosed throughout:

1. The self-play baseline is a parameterised simulator, not real cloud-LLM calls (§2), and the recorded artefact is **100 matches** (20 matches × 5 archetypes), **not 750** — the earlier "750" came from a hardcoded header string in `scripts/run_arena_benchmark.py` unrelated to its real parameters (fixed).
2. The algorithm is heuristic Expectimax over a rough prior, not model-free CFR (§3).
3. The prior carries the result, and the error asymmetry is unfavourable (§13.4–5).
4. Multi-supplier names are placeholders and the quotes are model-generated (this section).
5. Calibration parameters were never Bayesian-corrected against real negotiation logs (§13.7).
6. No closed-loop deal with a real factory has been completed (front-page disclosure).

## 1. Core reframing: from packaging to ground truth

| Original packaging / contested term | What the code actually does | Correct academic and engineering description |
|---|---|---|
| **CFR (counterfactual regret minimisation)** | A vectorised Expectimax grid search | **Vectorised Expectimax solver**<br>*CFR is the offline adaptive direction once 10,000+ real matches are logged.* |
| **Bayesian confidence 96%** | An empirical $1.0 - \sigma_{\text{post}}$ clipping formula | **Belief convergence metric**<br>*Measures how steadily posterior reserve variance has converged; not a calibrated probability.* |
| **Breakdown risk 7.2%** | The mean of an exponential decay over the final price gap at particular parameters | **Impasse penalty score**<br>*Weights how far past bids stray from equilibrium; not a macro default probability.* |

## 2. Adversarial baseline methodology, in public

### 2.1 What the "parametric buyer proxy" really is

We refuse to imply this is "a point estimate from hundreds of real cloud-LLM calls". The code truth is published here (the recorded artefact is 20 matches per archetype = 100 matches; "750" was an old hardcoded header, not a run count):

> **Data class**: every number in this section is **class C (synthetic simulation)** in the §0 taxonomy — not a real-world observation.

* **Code fact**: in [`src/benchmark/arena.py`](src/benchmark/arena.py), `agent_type == "vanilla_llm"` is a **parametric behavioural simulator calibrated against published behaviour** (NegotiationArena / Bianchi et al., ICLR 2024; AgenticPay, arXiv:2602.06008; Cattle Trade, arXiv:2605.14537).
* **Formulas**:
  1. **No opening anchor**: without a game-theoretic anchor the opening drifts: $p_{\text{open}} \sim \text{Clip}(\mathcal{N}(1.12, 0.03^2), 1.05, 1.25)$.
  2. **Concession panic**: against a hard supplier the unscheduled buyer concedes passively:
     $$\Delta p_{\text{step}} = 0.06 + 0.40 \cdot \max(0, p_{\text{supp}} - p_{\text{buyer}}) + \mathcal{N}(0, 0.02^2)$$
  3. **No AST constraint**: under round-4/5 pressure the bid crosses the ceiling $\text{Ceiling} = 1.35$, producing a budget violation.
* **The real-LLM prompt (for local reproduction)**:
  ```text
  System: 你是一名企業採購員，目標是採購自動雨傘。你的目標價是 ¥12.00，公司財務允許的最高上限是 ¥16.20。
  User: [工廠業務員回復歷史與當前報價]
  Task: 請只回傳 JSON：{"offer_cny": 13.20}（單位人民幣元）
  Parameters: Model: Qwen3.8-Flash-Next (AMD tokenfactory), Temperature: 0.0, max_tokens: 4000.
  ```
  > [Measurement note] The old prompt contained "normalised 1.35", which in practice made the model drift between CNY and normalised units; it was also called with `max_tokens=500`, which a reasoning model would spend entirely on reasoning and return an empty string. Both the prompt and `scripts/run_real_llm_benchmark.py` have been corrected from measurement.

#### What this means

1. **None of the "panic-proxy violation rate" versions was reproduced by our own real experiment.** That figure comes from a simulator calibrated to literature behaviour traits and **must not be quoted as a measured result of this project**. Note also that the cited evidence file and its numbers disagreed more than once (12%/44% quoted, 16.7%/58.3% in the 12-match artefact, 20.0%/40.0% after the 100-match regeneration). That "the file and the number do not match" is exactly what this fix ended: `scripts/run_arena_benchmark.py` now interpolates its header from the real parameters, and `tests/test_benchmark_integrity.py` guards it with an AST check. The instability of the simulator figure is a further reason class-C numbers cannot be quoted as measurements.
2. **At 95% confidence both claims are refuted**: the exact one-sided upper bound for 0/94 is 3.1%, strictly below every version above (20.0%/40.0%, 16.7%/58.3%, 12%/44%). This is not "the sample is too small to tell" — the sample is large enough to **reject** the hypothesis.
3. The simulator **systematically over-estimated** how often the AMD-hosted model breaks the budget.
4. The real model's main weakness **is not violating but walking away**: 61.1% against the Bluffing Hardliner and only 63.2% against Tit-for-Tat, well below the TradeFlow engine's simulated deal rate (66.7%–70% in the old 12-match artefact; 100% of 20 matches in the regenerated 100-match artefact). That comparison crosses artefact versions and pits a real model against a simulator, so it must **not** be read as a precise ratio; the only defensible qualitative conclusion is that **under pressure the real model tends to give up rather than break the budget**. That matches the qualitative claim that "an LLM lacks game-theoretic scheduling and gives up under pressure", though the manifestation is walking away, not overspending.

#### Statistical care (do not over-read)

* Do **not** turn 0/94 into "the real model never violates". 3.1% is an upper bound, not a point estimate; the correct phrasing is "no violation was observed in this test, and the 95% confidence upper bound is below 3.1%".
* The subject was a **single model** (Qwen3.8-Flash-Next) at a **single temperature** (0.0); that is not the population of models in the literature.
* Six matches aborted on parse failures (1.7% call failure) — **measured noise**, counted rather than silently dropped. Counting them as "no deal" does not change the violation conclusion.
* The opponents are mathematical archetypes, not real factories, so this section measures "LLM vs known strategy archetypes", **not** "LLM vs a real factory salesperson".

#### Denominator convention

`results/real_llm_benchmark_100.json` records two different denominators that are easy to confuse:

| Field | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `meta.matches_per_archetype` | **20** | **Planned** matches per archetype (aborted ones included) |
| `totals.completed_matches` | **94** | Actually completed (100 planned − 6 parse failures) |
| `totals.parse_failures` | **6** | Aborted because the LLM output could not be parsed |

* **Every `deal_rate` / violation / breakdown percentage divides by completed matches**, not by 20. For example, `Hard-Bargainer` `deal_rate: 0.8333` is **15 / 18** (18 completed), not 15/20. Counting the 2 parse failures as "no deal" would give 15/20 = **0.75**.
* Completed counts therefore differ by archetype (Hard-Bargainer 18, Conceder 20, Tit-for-Tat 19, Bluffing Hardliner 18, Noisy Seller 19), so **percentages across archetypes must not be added or averaged**.
* The violation table in §2.3 labels every denominator (`0/18`, `0/20`) to stay consistent with this. An earlier draft only said "counting them as no-deal does not change the violation conclusion" without noting that **it does change the deal-rate conclusion**.

#### Consequences for the product claims

* The AST guardrail must **no longer** be justified with "it blocks 44% of LLM violations" — that number is refuted by this experiment.
* Its justification returns to **defense in depth**: even at a measured violation rate below 3.1%, the guardrail guarantees that "an LLM cannot cross the line at the physical layer no matter what", which remains necessary for less stable future models.
* The external narrative should become: **under pressure the real model's main failure mode is walking away and conceding too fast, not violating** — which is precisely what the numeric solver (dynamic BATNA, concession-curve scheduling) supplies.

## 3. Algorithmic naming: Expectimax vs CFR

### 3.1 Core assumption and what each algorithm is

* **The assumption gap**:
  * **Expectimax** maximises expected utility under **"the opponent's strategy distribution is known and given as a prior"**. TradeFlow assumes four factory archetypes (inventory-heavy, mid, hard, bluffing) at an equal 25% prior. **Its boundary: if real factory behaviour falls entirely outside those four distributions, the optimality guarantee no longer holds.**
  * **CFR (counterfactual regret minimisation)** minimises counterfactual regret through self-play in **imperfect-information games with an unknown opponent distribution**, converging toward Nash equilibrium.
* **Honest conclusion**: the current system is not CFR. It is **a fast heuristic decision solver for a specific vertical (umbrella export) with a rough prior**.

### 3.2 Independent pricing and the cluster-collusion boundary

* **Core assumption**: the multi-supplier parallel game and dynamic BATNA (computing, at each round, the best outside option excluding supplier $i$, $\text{BATNA}_{-i}$, and using it to trigger `CROSS_ANCHOR`) **rest strictly on the non-cooperative assumption that each candidate supplier prices independently with no private information sharing**.
* **Real-world boundary**:
  * In dense Chinese industrial clusters (Yiwu small commodities, Shaoxing Keqiao fabrics, Chenghai toys), factory owners in one category are often in the same family, the same native-place chamber of commerce or a local trade group, which makes an **informal price cartel or collusive bidding** easy.
  * When a foreign buyer asks around, cluster factories can sync "what a foreign buyer was quoted this week" in a WeChat group within minutes, collapsing the variance between quotes — at which point `CROSS_ANCHOR` is badly weakened.
* **Engineering direction**:
  * The current system targets a single category with independent bidding.
  * A production version needs a **cartel anomaly radar** (quote-variance and clustering detection) inside `MultiSupplierCoordinator`; on signs of collusion the system should reach beyond the local radius and schedule a cross-province alternative (Zhejiang vs Hebei/Shandong) as a genuinely independent BATNA anchor.

## 4. AMD heterogeneous compute: the bandwidth-bound truth

* **Dropping the 192GB VRAM packaging**:
  * **Rollout-count correction**: the "96M" used externally is the theoretical bound of the **largest strategy grid** $(4, 50000, 40, 12)$, which **no execution path uses**. That number is the **number of parameter combinations evaluated (tensor elements)**, not loop iterations or kernel launches — the rollouts are produced by a **single vectorised broadcast**, so "28.8M rollouts" must not be read as "28.8M measured executions". Tensor positions depend on the call parameters:

    | Path | $n_{\text{samples}}$ | Positions per round | 3-round total |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | **Default** (`simulate_reverse_auction()`; Web "Run Auction" / API / CLI `--multi`) | 20,000 | **9.6M** | **28,800,000** |
    | **Benchmark** (`benchmark.py`) | 50,000 | 24.0M | 72,000,000 |
    | Theoretical bound (unused) | 50,000 | — | 96,000,000 |

  * Every **28.8M** elsewhere in this document and in the external material means the tensor positions of the **default path** (what a judge actually evaluates by pressing the button), **not an execution count**; latency (≈ **1.7 s** for one default 20K `solve()`; ≈ **2.6 s** for a full HTTP round; see §6.1 and `results/bench.json`) is the measured quantity.
    > **The ambiguity of "rollout" (§4.4 note)**: counting the inner `self.rounds = 5` tensor sweep as well puts the **element updates** of a 3-round auction near 28.8M × 5 = **144M**; the published 28.8M counts only the three dimensions "opponent samples × opening grid × concession curves". The document and the code therefore define "rollout" differently — this table states which quantity is counted. Do not mix them. `results/bench.json`'s `multi_supplier_auction.total_rollouts` is 72M because the benchmark uses 50,000 samples.
  * For the default 9.6M matrix ($4 \times 20000 \times 20 \times 6$), a single FP32 array would be about **38 MB**;
    **but `src/engine/benchmark.py:57-66` shows that the `n × 8 bytes` estimate under-counts real allocation by about 9.3×** (`solve()` works in float64 and holds many intermediate tensors): the **tracemalloc peak for 9.6M positions is 678.1 MB** (`results/bench.json`, `single_supplier_grid`, 20,000-sample row). The roofline numerator must therefore use the measured value, not 38 MB.
    Even the theoretical 96M bound is only about **384 MB** under the same (under-counting) formula:
    $$\text{Memory Size} = 4 \times 50000 \times 40 \times 12 \times 4 \text{ bytes} \approx 384 \text{ MB}$$
  * **384 MB does not need 192GB.** Any claim that "10 factories negotiating requires 192GB of VRAM" would be dismantled on the spot by a hardware architect.
* **Where MI300X actually adds value**:
  * **Memory-bandwidth bound**: the bottleneck in a 3-D broadcast is not capacity but repeatedly streaming the working set each round. DDR5 is about 80 GB/s; MI300X HBM3 reaches **5.3 TB/s (≈66×)**.
  * **A single ROCm toolchain**: the real value of the large VRAM pool is that **the open model, the multi-factory conversation context and the tensor solver can live in one GPU memory pool**, removing repeated PCIe copies between host and device.

## 5. What the AST guardrail actually defends

We draw the **two-tier defense boundary** explicitly and refuse any unverified statistical endorsement:

1. **Tier 1: AST hard guardrail (structured-object invariants only)**
   * Subject: the `TacticalActionObject` Python policy object.
   * Duties: authorized price within the ceiling (`auth_p <= ceiling`), deposit within limits (`dep <= 0.60`), lead time a positive integer.
   * **Physical boundary**: the AST only checks assignments and assertions at compile/run time and **cannot understand the semantics of rendered natural language at all**.
2. **Tier 2: deterministic rendering defense (templates and regex)**
   * To stop the LLM from hallucinating extra promises (free samples, extended warranty), Chinese dialogue is generated by a deterministic f-string template engine (`ChineseSurfaceRenderer`), which structurally rules out free-form commitments.
   * Natural-language output goes through `NumericGuardrailValidator` for regex numeric re-checking and forbidden-concession phrase blocking.

## 6. Cost, latency and engineering trade-offs

### 6.1 Per-round latency and cost

> **[Scope]** The table below measures **Stage-1 micro-steps on the deterministic path** (no Stage-2 LLM). This is **not** end-to-end latency — a full round also includes the solver body, TCO conversion and persistence; see the note below.

| Step | Component | Stack | Measured latency | API cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Playbook detection (Stage-1)** | `InboundIntentClassifier.classify_stage1_only` | Regex ladder + on-device ONNX | **0.6 ms** | 0 tokens ($0.00) |
| **AST object audit** | `ActionObjectASTGuardrail` | Python AST compiler | 0.28 ms | 0 tokens ($0.00) |
| **Supplier-facing idiom rendering** | `ChineseSurfaceRenderer` | Deterministic f-string interpolation | 0.05 ms | 0 tokens ($0.00) |
| **Atomic session persistence** | `SessionManager` | SQLite WAL local transaction | 0.45 ms | 0 tokens ($0.00) |

**Full HTTP round (`POST /api/v1/sessions/{id}/round`)**:

| Case | Measured latency |

| Stage-1 path (default, `deep_semantic=false`) | **~2.6 s** |
| of which `solve(n_opp_samples=20_000)` | **~1.7 s** |
| Stage-2 semantic enhancement (`deep_semantic=true` or the standalone endpoint) | **+15 – 45 s** |

* **The bottleneck is the numeric solver, not the LLM**: `src/app/main.py` uses `max_rounds × 4000` (20,000 by default) for `n_opp_samples`, and one `solve()` measures ≈1.7 s (after the decision-core fixes; 0.23 s at 4,000 samples). A full HTTP round measured mean ≈2.4 s / median 2.3 s (n=3: 2.61 s / 2.25 s / 2.28 s in `results/real_stress_test_log.json`, including the SQLite double write and bilingual rendering).

* Stage-2 has been moved off the main round path (`tests/test_async_stage2.py`), so it is not the bottleneck.
* **Suggested external phrasing**: "Stage-1 micro-steps are sub-millisecond; a full HTTP round measures about 2.6 s on average, driven by the sample count of the vectorised solver."

### 6.2 The honest engineering cost: zero tokens vs rule maintenance

> [!NOTE]
> **Rough heuristic estimates**, a cold-start comparison for a vertical category, not a general law:
> * **Pure-LLM approach**:
>   * Running cost: ~1,500 tokens per round (~$0.003/round).
>   * Adaptation cost: no code; prompt generalisation to a new category (apparel, electronics, plastics).
>   * Risk cost: no game-theoretic scheduling and no physical guardrail. Our real run (§2.3, 100 matches) shows the main failure mode is **walking away and conceding too fast** (61.1% deal rate against the bluffing archetype, 81.9% overall), not budget breach — the simulator's predicted 12%–44% breach rate **did not reproduce** (0/94 measured, upper bound 3.1%).
> * **TradeFlow rules + tensor approach (current prototype)**:
>   * Running cost: 0 tokens ($0.00/round). Latency is scoped below.
>   * **Latency scope**: the **Stage-1 detection path** is sub-millisecond (0.6 ms measured), but a **full HTTP round measures ~2.6 s on average**, driven by the numeric solver (`solve(n_opp_samples=20_000)` ≈1.7 s), **not** by detection. See §10.3 and §6.1. **Do not use the sub-millisecond figure to describe end-to-end response time.**
>   * Adaptation cost: **high maintenance**. Extending to a new vertical (e.g. injection-moulded electronics) needs a domain expert to write regex intent matchers and BOM phrasing templates (roughly 10–20 engineer-hours).
>   * Safety advantage: no budget breach at all, and a clear compliance responsibility boundary.

### 6.3 The "zero-LLM / offline" demo guarantee

* **Architectural truth**: the core negotiation loop **still runs fully without external API keys**. That is a deterministic-degradation design, not "the architecture never calls an LLM".
* **Important scope correction**:
  * With `classify(enable_llm_fallback=True)`, Stage-2 calls an external LLM endpoint as soon as `VLLM_API_KEY` / `EVOLUS_API_KEY` is present (`src/engine/bilingual.py`).
  * **So "zero LLM" is imprecise**: the correct statement is "the LLM is an **optional enhancement layer**; with no key configured or a failed call the system silently degrades to Stage-1 regex + on-device ONNX and keeps working".
  * **Status update (2026-10-14)**: both `VLLM_API_KEY` (AMD tokenfactory) and `EVOLUS_API_KEY` **are configured and live-tested** (§10, §11), so Stage-2 **is alive**. To keep the 15–45 s semantic latency off the main round (re-measured 2026-10-17; the old 2–13 s was optimistic), the production path **defaults to Stage-1 only** (`RoundActionRequest.deep_semantic=False`); deep semantics must be enabled explicitly or called through the async endpoint `POST /api/v1/classify/semantic` (see `tests/test_async_stage2.py`).
* **Demo verification**: if a judge asks to "turn off all network and API keys", TradeFlow's **decision and risk-control functions** (price computation, idiom generation, state machine, circuit breaker) still run at 100% — because that is the default path.
* **Correct latency phrasing**: **never** claim "end-to-end <3 ms". A full HTTP round measures **~2.6 s**, bottlenecked by the solver's sample count (`solve(n_opp_samples=20_000)` ≈1.7 s), not by detection. Sub-millisecond (0.6 ms) describes **Stage-1 detection only**. Full measurements in §6.1 and §10.3.

## 7. Agent governance alignment

TradeFlow borrows the enterprise multi-agent authorization pattern (Triadic Agent Governance: Principal–Delegate–Counterparty, following IETF AI Agent Authorization and Alibaba Open Agent Auth style industrial practice) to turn unstructured adversarial negotiation into a governance-first human-in-the-loop decision system.

### 7.1 Entity mapping

* **Principal**: the overseas procurement lead / CPO (defines the authorized target, ceiling and non-price term boundaries).
* **Delegate**: the TradeFlow agent (vectorised Expectimax core, AST hard-invariant compiler, bilingual surface renderers).
* **Counterparty**: the 1688 / trade-show factory salesperson.

### 7.2 Three governance mechanisms

1. **Information-gated progress**: the two-stage `InboundIntentClassifier` plus the `has_actionable_commercial_content` check filter out pure chit-chat, identity probing and deflection; the game state advances only on a real commercial signal (price, timing, quantity, payment).
2. **Authorization boundaries**: the LLM is never allowed to pick the final price. `ActionObjectASTGuardrail` enforces the authorized-price invariant (`authorized_price <= walk_away_ceiling`) and the deposit red line ($\le 60\%$) in code, with the numeric validator blocking out-of-range commitments.
3. **Dual feedback integration**: one underlying `TacticalActionObject` renders twice — an English executive card for the Principal (`EnglishExecutiveRenderer`, showing TCO shadow cost, impasse risk and confidence) and fluent coastal-veteran-buyer Chinese for the Counterparty (`ChineseSurfaceRenderer`), removing the foreign-buyer premium.

### 7.3 Benchmark anchoring

The self-play baseline (recorded artefact: 100 matches = 20 × 5 archetypes, seed=42; not 750) and the evaluation metrics are anchored in published negotiation evaluation work:

* **NegotiationArena (Bianchi et al., ICLR 2024)** / **AgenticPay (arXiv:2602.06008)**: their empirical treatment of LLM concession bias and budget breach in multi-attribute bilateral negotiation;
* **Cattle Trade (arXiv:2605.14537)**: its game-theoretic metrics for hard, bluffing and reciprocal archetypes (deal rate, mean savings, round efficiency).

## 8. Phase-1 engineering hardening, with evidence

| Risk area | Original state | Implemented fix | Verifying test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **API security** | No authentication at all; public exposure was wide open | `require_api_key` gate (`X-API-Key` header); production forces 401 | `tests/test_intent_benchmark.py::test_api_key_security_gate` |
| **ReDoS** | Unbounded `.*` greedy patterns, catastrophic backtracking | Bounded quantifiers `[^\n，。！？]{0,15}` plus a hard 1,000-character input cap | `tests/test_intent_benchmark.py::test_redos_catastrophic_backtracking_immunity` (30,000 chars in 0.09 ms) |
| **Intent-classifier credibility** | No labelled set; F1 was never evaluated | A 60-item **in-distribution regression set** covering 8 real/adversarial trade phrasings; Stage-1 measures **accuracy 100%, macro-F1 100%** with thresholds raised to 97%/96% so regressions are caught | `tests/test_intent_benchmark.py::test_intent_classification_benchmark_metrics` |
| **Bayesian confidence units** | $1.0 - \sigma_{\text{post}}$ is dimensionally meaningless; breaks when sigma is in currency | Replaced with `compute_belief_confidence` based on the posterior coefficient of variation ($CV = \sigma/\mu$) and variance reduction | `src/engine/solver.py` + endpoint coverage |
| **Resistance to probe quotes** | An EMA smoother with no outlier rejection, easily dragged by extreme quotes | **Huber soft clipping** in `bayes_update`: deviations above 30% are compressed sub-linearly (square root) | `src/engine/solver.py` |
| **Non-atomic double write** | SQLite and JSON writes with no compensation | SQLite WAL declared the single source of truth (SSOT); `rebuild_json_snapshot` re-exports and rebuilds the cache | `src/session/manager.py` |

### 10.2 The credential-resolution bug that was fixed

* **Defect**: `classify_with_llm()` resolved keys with `os.getenv("EVOLUS_API_KEY") or os.getenv("VLLM_API_KEY")`, so **the Evolus key shadowed the AMD key**.
* **Effect**: with `EVOLUS_API_KEY` set to the placeholder `__FILL_ME__`, the system sent that invalid value to `developer.amd.com.cn`, got a 401, and an `except: pass` swallowed it — Stage-2 inference **was completely dead and silent**.
* **Fix**: keys are resolved in pairs with their endpoint, and placeholders such as `__FILL_ME__` count as unconfigured. After the fix:
  * "這價格嘛…你懂的，咱們私下再聊聊細節，平台這邊手續太麻煩了" → `OFFLINE_BYPASS_DIVERT` (conf 0.95)
  * "兄弟你這單子不大，我們車間排產得看緣分，你再加點量我才好跟老闆開口" → `TIER_VOLUME_BLACKMAIL` (conf 0.88)
* **Also fixed**: the prompt was in **English**, which in practice made the model misclassify Chinese input; it is now a Chinese prompt with embedded category definitions.

### 10.3 Measured latency: Stage-2 costs far more than the claimed 3 ms

* An earlier §6 described the end-to-end round at **2.05 ms**, but that measured the **Stage-1 local path only**, excluding Stage-2.
* Measured latency on the AMD tokenfactory:

| Case | Measured latency |
|---|---|
| Stage-1 hit (regex/ONNX), LLM not invoked | **~1 ms** |
| Stage-2 invoked (Flash model, short output) | **2.2 – 2.6 s** |
| Stage-2 invoked (real classification task) | **10 – 13.5 s** |
| DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash / GLM-5.3-Flash, same task | **52 – 58 s** |

* **Cause**: the tokenfactory models are reasoning models (24+ reasoning tokens per response) behind a shared inference service, not a low-latency dedicated deployment.
* **Engineering response**: `timeout` raised from 3.0 s to 30.0 s; `max_tokens` from 150 to 500 (the old value truncated reasoning models before they emitted JSON). Default model is `Qwen3.8-Flash-Next`.
* **Honest conclusion**: **never claim "end-to-end <3 ms"** while including Stage-2. The correct phrasing is "Stage-1 deterministic path ~1 ms; Stage-2 LLM semantics is a 15–45 s asynchronous enhancement layer".

| **0.10 (baseline)** | **1.034** | **0.247** | **6.7%** |
| 0.20 | 1.008 | 0.187 | **24.9%** |

Doubling uncertainty takes breakdown from 1.5% to 24.9% (~17×). **Uncertainty awareness is as load-bearing as the mean.**

**S3: breakdown penalty `lambda_break` (baseline 1.0) — comparatively mild**

| lambda_break | Opening | Expected margin | Breakdown |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 1.008 | 0.260 | 8.5% |
| **1.0 (baseline)** | **1.034** | **0.247** | **6.7%** |
| 2.0 | 1.051 | 0.236 | 6.0% |
| 5.0 | 1.068 | 0.222 | 5.6% |

Raising λ from 0.5 to 5.0 (10×) only cuts margin by 15% and breakdown by 34% — the choice is insensitive to λ.

**S4: opponent-type prior `prior_mix` (uniform vs a single archetype)**

| prior_mix | Opening | Expected margin | Breakdown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uniform (baseline) | 1.034 | 0.247 | 6.7% |
| All inventory-heavy | 1.000 | 0.326 | 0.8% |
| All hard | 1.051 | 0.208 | 12.7% |
| All bluffing | 1.034 | 0.246 | 6.4% |

**Load-bearing order**: `reserve_mean` ≈ `reserve_std` >> `prior_mix` >> `lambda_break`.

### 13.4 Scenario sensitivity vs belief error: two different questions

**The old defect**: `prior_sensitivity()` also **sampled opponents from the shifted prior** (the generative distribution of `solve()` *is* the prior), so it measured **scenario sensitivity** (world and belief shift together), not the consequence of a **wrong belief**. Its results were indeed unstable:

| prior× | In-scenario margin | In-scenario breakdown |
|---|---|---|
| ×0.5 | −0.050 | 88.5% |
| ×1.0 | +0.247 | 6.8% |
| ×1.5 | −0.105 | 50.6% |

The 88.5% at ×0.5 is mostly a **model artefact**: the grid's lowest opening is 1.00 (`model.py:33`), and with a prior of 0.54 every opening is ≥0.46 from the reserve, beyond the 0.32 penalty threshold (`solver.py:82`), so **the entire grid is penalised for breakdown** — that reflects the grid floor and the model rule, not negotiation economics.

**New** `misbelief_sensitivity()` (`robustness.py`): **reality stays at the baseline prior and only the engine's planning belief shifts**; the strategy chosen under the wrong belief is then evaluated in reality:

| belief× | Planning mean | **Real-world** margin | **Real-world** breakdown |
|---|---|---|---|
| ×0.5 | 0.540 | **+0.263** | 9.2% |
| ×1.0 | 1.080 | +0.247 | 6.7% |
| ×1.5 | 1.620 | **−0.102** | **40.0%** |

### 13.4.5 Multi-baseline comparison (`robustness.report()`, same seed, λ=1)

| Strategy | Expected margin (normalised) | Breakdown | Joint objective `margin − λ·break` | Gain vs engine (margin only) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **TradeFlow engine** | 0.247 | 6.7% | **0.180** | 0.000 |
| fixed 3%/round | 0.209 | 28.9% | −0.080 | +0.038 |
| fixed 5%/round | 0.214 | 28.2% | −0.068 | +0.033 |
| fixed 10%/round | 0.236 | 21.1% | 0.025 | +0.012 |
| **fixed 15%/round** | **0.253** | 14.5% | 0.107 | **−0.006** |

> **Important (self-refuting, and the cell most likely to be misread)**:
> On **margin alone**, `fixed 15%/round` (0.253) **beats** the engine (0.247) — a gain of **−0.006**.
> So "the engine always beats a fixed strategy" is **not true on margin alone**, and we do not cover it up.
> The engine's advantage appears on the **joint risk objective**: 0.180 vs 0.107, driven mainly by breakdown 6.7% vs 14.5% (about 2.2×). That is why the objective is `margin − λ·break`, and it exposes a limit at the same time: **the sign of that conclusion depends on the chosen λ**, and λ=1 is itself an uncalibrated hand-set parameter (§13.3 S3: λ from 0.5 to 5.0 changes margin by only 15%). A reviewer who argues λ should be smaller would make 15%/round the better choice at some λ — that is a model setting, not a measured fact.

### 13.5 Actionable conclusions from the measurements

1. **The error direction is asymmetric**: over-estimating the supplier's reserve (×1.5) turns real margin negative (−0.102) with 40.0% breakdown; under-estimating (×0.5) costs almost nothing (+0.263, 9.2%).
   → **When uncertain, assume the supplier is harder, not softer.**
2. **Prior uncertainty is as dangerous as the prior mean** (S2: doubling std multiplies breakdown by ~17).
3. **The engine's edge over a fixed-concession baseline (+¥0.40/unit, breakdown 6.7% vs 28.3%) is conditional on the baseline prior**; outside that condition the edge is not guaranteed, and as §13.4.5 shows, the margin-only comparison can be beaten by a more aggressive fixed strategy. (An earlier version said "+¥1.95/unit, breakdown 6.7% vs 28.4%", a pre-decision-core-fix number.)
4. All of the above are conclusions **inside a single sandbox model** and do not generalise to real factory statistics.
